Why do I snore all of a sudden? 12 causes by onset speed

By Mikhail Manev· Published September 11, 2026· 10 min read

Snoring that starts suddenly almost always has a cause that started just as suddenly: a drink, a sedating pill, a cold, a new bed that puts you on your back, or a short night. Slower causes, weight, pregnancy, hormones and age, take weeks to years and rarely feel sudden. In one Swedish maternity ward, 23% of women snored every night in the last week of pregnancy, against 4% before it (Franklin et al. 2000). The fast causes are the ones a tagged recording can isolate in a week or two.

Key facts
  • Alcohol reduced upper-airway stability in a dose-dependent way in every snorer tested, and the airway was most collapsible in REM sleep (Issa and Sullivan 1984).
  • A 10% weight gain predicted a 32% rise in apneas and hypopneas per hour and a six-fold increase in the odds of developing moderate to severe sleep-disordered breathing (Peppard et al. 2000, 690 adults over 4 years).
  • 23% of women reported snoring every night in the last week of pregnancy, against 4% before pregnancy (Franklin et al. 2000, 502 women).
  • Postmenopausal women had 2.6 times the odds of sleep-disordered breathing of premenopausal women, after adjusting for age and body habitus (Young et al. 2003, Wisconsin Sleep Cohort).
  • Snorly tags nights with alcohol, late meals, back sleeping and congestion and compares snore minutes with and without each tag. No Snorly data on sudden-onset snoring yet; the numbers here are from the studies cited.

Why do I snore some nights and not others?

Because the fast causes change nightly. Alcohol, a late meal, a sedating tablet, a blocked nose and which way you fell asleep can each turn a silent night into a loud one, and be gone the next. Snoring that comes and goes is good news: a trigger, not a fixed narrowing, is doing the work, and triggers can be removed.

The mechanism is the same for every cause, relaxed tissue vibrating in a narrowed airway, as described in why do I snore. What differs is how quickly each cause arrives, and that is the fastest way to sort them.

What can make you snore overnight?

Five things, and you probably know which one applied last night.

Alcohol. It relaxes the muscles that hold the throat open. Issa and Sullivan measured how much suction it took to close the airway in sleeping snorers; alcohol reduced airway stability in all of them, more with a larger dose. Alcohol nights are loud in the first half and settle toward morning; the hours-before-bed question is in alcohol and snoring.

Sedatives, sleeping pills and sedating antihistamines. Benzodiazepines, z-drugs and first-generation antihistamines such as diphenhydramine relax the same muscles. In an anaesthesia study, an intravenous benzodiazepine made 7 of 8 healthy men snore (Masuda et al. 1995). A bedtime dose is far smaller, but the direction is the same.

A cold or an allergy flare. A blocked nose forces mouth breathing, the jaw drops, the tongue slides back. Nasal snoring is higher and thinner, and it changes the night the cold arrives.

A new bed, pillow or hotel. Anything that lands you on your back. In Nakano's 72 habitual snorers, most of those without apnea snored less on their side than on their back. Position is covered in which sleeping position stops snoring.

Exhaustion. Mayo Clinic lists sleep deprivation as a cause: after a short night the throat relaxes further in the deep sleep that follows.

What makes you start snoring over a few weeks?

Weight gain. Fat around the neck narrows the airway from outside. In 690 Wisconsin adults measured four years apart, a 10% weight gain predicted a 32% rise in breathing events per hour and six-fold odds of moderate to severe sleep-disordered breathing (Peppard et al. 2000). Snoring tends to move first.

Nasal polyps or a lingering sinus infection. Congestion that outlasts the cold. If one side of your nose has been blocked for three weeks, this is the suspect.

Pregnancy. Weight, fluid and a swollen nasal lining all narrow the airway. Franklin's 502 women went from 4% nightly snorers before pregnancy to 23% in the last week, and snoring went with higher rates of pregnancy hypertension, so mention it to your midwife. More in snoring in women.

A new medication. Muscle relaxants, some antidepressants, some blood-pressure drugs that congest the nose. If the snoring began within two weeks of a new prescription, ask the prescriber before stopping anything.

What makes you start snoring over months or years?

Age and muscle tone. Throat muscle tone falls through the 40s and 50s. In Young's 1993 sample of 602 working adults aged 30 to 60, sleep-disordered breathing was already present in 9% of women and 24% of men, most often in habitual snorers.

Menopause. Postmenopausal women had 2.6 times the odds of sleep-disordered breathing of premenopausal women after adjustment (Young et al. 2003), and Bixler found sleep apnea in 2.7% of postmenopausal women not on hormone therapy against 0.6% of premenopausal women. Snoring that begins around 50 in a lifelong non-snorer is common and worth taking seriously.

Hypothyroidism. An underactive thyroid is a recognised risk factor for sleep-disordered breathing, although the evidence is inconsistent and the studies small (Kuczyński et al. 2016). New snoring plus tiredness, cold intolerance and weight gain is a reason for a thyroid blood test.

How do tagged nights find the trigger in two weeks?

By turning "I think it is the wine" into two columns of numbers. Record every night with the phone in the same spot, 0.5 to 1 m from your head, and before you sleep tag what applied: alcohol, late meal, back sleeping, congestion, a pill. After 10 to 14 nights, compare snore minutes on nights with a tag against nights without it. The tag whose nights are clearly louder is your trigger.

Snorly does the comparison for you: it records only when it detects snoring, keeps snore minutes and a nightly score, and lines tagged nights up against untagged ones. Any recorder with a nightly summary and a notebook works the same way. Change one thing per night, never two. If every night is loud whatever you tag, the cause is a slow one: stop chasing triggers and look at weight, hormones, age or the nose.

When is sudden snoring a reason to see a doctor?

Three patterns. First, snoring that stops for 10 seconds or more and restarts with a gasp, the pattern NHLBI describes for obstructive sleep apnea. A recording can capture it but cannot tell you whether your oxygen dropped; see snoring vs sleep apnea. Second, new loud snoring together with new daytime sleepiness; loud nightly snoring is tied to sleepiness even without apnea, as explained in does snoring mean deep sleep. Third, snoring in a child: the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that every child be screened for snoring, because in children it is usually tonsils and adenoids and is treatable.

None of these mean you have a condition. They mean the recording has done its job and the next listener should be a professional.

What to do tonight
  1. Record tonight as a baseline: phone face down on the nightstand, 0.5 to 1 m away, on the charger, Do Not Disturb on.
  2. Tag the night before you sleep: alcohol (how much, when), dinner time, any pill, congestion, back or side.
  3. Write down when the snoring started and what changed that week: a cold, a prescription, a new mattress, a holiday.
  4. In the morning, listen to the loudest clips for silences of 10 seconds or more that end in a gasp.
  5. Keep going for 14 nights, one change at a time, and compare tagged nights against untagged ones.

Related questions

Why do I snore now when I never used to?

Look for what changed and when. Same week: alcohol, a sedating pill, a cold, a new bed. Recent months: weight, pregnancy, a new prescription. Around 50: menopause and muscle tone. Fourteen tagged, recorded nights sort the fast causes from the slow ones.

Can a cold make you snore?

Yes, and it is one of the commonest reasons for sudden snoring. A blocked nose forces mouth breathing and the tongue drops back. The snoring sounds higher and more nasal, and it fades as the cold does. If it has not faded in three weeks, get the nose looked at.

Why do I snore after drinking?

Alcohol relaxes the muscles that hold the throat open, so the airway collapses further with each breath. In sleeping snorers the effect grew with the dose. The snoring is worst in the first hours after falling asleep and eases as the alcohol wears off.

Does sudden snoring mean sleep apnea?

Not on its own. Most sudden snoring has a trigger like alcohol, congestion or position. The sign that points toward apnea is snoring that stops for 10 seconds or more and restarts with a gasp, especially with daytime sleepiness. A recording can show the pattern; only a sleep study can confirm it.
